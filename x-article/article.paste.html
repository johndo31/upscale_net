Growing a model is free and exact, but the new capacity starts empty: even after a billion tokens of training, it bought only a rounding error over the small model.

Qwen2.5-0.5B grown to 1B, healed on a billion tokens against an honest control, for about $77.

By Niklas, drniklas.com. I also host the Venture Grade podcast.

The question

"Sparks of Weight-Space Programming", by Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab at MIT CSAIL, shows a frontier LLM reading and writing neural network weights directly, with no gradient descent. It made me wonder: can you "program" a small model into a big one? Take a model off Hugging Face, make it twice the size by computation rather than by training, and does that buy you anything?

Short answer: the growing part is easy and exact, and has nothing to do with LLMs. The hard part is what comes after, and at the budget I could afford, the honest answer to "does it buy anything" is: a rounding error.

Growing without changing anything

The trick is old (Net2Net, 2015) and simple. Every new piece of the network is initialised so it contributes exactly zero:

  • new residual dimensions are zero-padded; the layers that write into them (embedding, attention output, MLP output) get zero weights there, the layers that read from them get random weights, which read zeros
  • new attention heads and MLP neurons get random input weights and zero output weights
  • new layers are copies of a neighbour with their output projections zeroed, so they are the identity on the residual stream
  • RMSNorm averages over the hidden size, so zero-padding changes its scale by d/D; multiplying the norm weights by sqrt(d/D) and the epsilon by d/D cancels that exactly

Result: Qwen2.5-0.5B (hidden 896, 24 layers, 14 heads) becomes a 1.05B model (hidden 1280, 28 layers, 20 heads) whose logits match the original to float32 rounding (max difference 6e-5, identical greedy output on every prompt). It takes a minute on a laptop. The same code grows DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B to the exact Llama-3-8B shape in three minutes; I did not have the budget to heal that one.

So the 1B is exactly as smart as the 0.5B. Now what?

The new capacity is empty. Filling it means training, which the growth literature calls healing. Two things matter here that I think most write-ups skip:

  1. The control. Any model improves if you train it on another billion tokens. The only fair comparison is the small model trained on exactly the same tokens with the same settings. If grown-and-healed does not beat that, growth bought nothing.
  2. Equal tokens is not equal cost. The small model trains 1.6x faster per token. A compute-matched control would see 1.6 billion tokens in the same GPU hours. I ran the token-matched control and report both framings.

Setup: 1B tokens of fineweb-edu, AdamW, peak learning rate 3e-5 (1e-4 damaged the pretrained weights within 500 steps), cosine to 10%, 65k-token batches, one H100. Benchmarks with lm-eval: hellaswag, arc, piqa, winogrande, wikitext perplexity, full task sets, plus five real 1B-class models for scale. Every number below comes from a single training run per variant, one seed.

What this says:

  • At equal tokens, growth wins by 0.006 nats of held-out loss and by nothing on benchmarks. Every benchmark difference between the grown model and the control is within the noise of the tasks. Twice the parameters, same tokens, same model as far as the tasks can tell.
  • At equal compute, growth probably loses. The control finished in 5.7 hours, the grown model in 9. Give the small model the same 9 hours and it sees 1.6B tokens, and its loss was still falling when it stopped. I did not run that comparison, so this is an expectation, not a measurement.
  • The curve shows why the gain is small. The control leads for the first 10,000 steps, because it has no random new units adding noise to a tuned network. The grown model catches up around step 10,000 and pulls away by 0.006 over the last third. More capacity shows up late, and slowly.

[IMAGE 04-crossover.png]

  • Both runs got worse at Wikipedia. Two points of wikitext perplexity, identical for control and grown. That is fineweb-edu pulling the model toward web text, not an effect of growth.
  • A billion tokens is nothing. The best current models in this size class (OLMo-2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, SmolLM2-1.7B) are 10 to 20 points ahead. They were trained on trillions of tokens, so one billion is well under 0.1% of their budget. Size alone is not the story either: Pythia-1B, twice the source's size, scores below it.

Can the empty part be filled with something?

Before the long run I spent about $21 on four short pilots: four ways to initialise the new capacity, each trained on the first 131M tokens of the same 1B schedule, so the winner could simply be resumed. The rule for picking was fixed in advance: lowest held-out loss at step 2,000, ties within 0.01 go to the simplest option.

  • Zero: random reader, zero writer. Empty.
  • LLM plan: written by an LLM from weight and activation statistics.
  • Split: Net2Net. Copy a neuron and halve both outputs.
  • Guided: split, but the busiest units go first.

[IMAGE 05-pilots.png]

Empty won the pick, but only its lead over split and guided means much. The plan's 0.002 deficit is a draw, inside the tie margin and from one seed. Two things I learned from these runs are, I think, the most useful part of this post.

[IMAGE 02-constructions.png]

Exact splits make twins. A split copies a neuron's reader and halves both writers, which preserves the function. But the two copies then see the same input and have the same writer, so they get the same gradient, forever. I measured it on the split model after 131M tokens: in the four layers I checked (0, 10, 20, 23), the mean cosine between twin neurons is 1.0000 for all three weight matrices, against 0.007 for random pairs. That checkpoint is stored in bf16, so differences too small for bf16 would not show. To that precision they have not separated. The twins are about 40% of the new parameters, so a split model is roughly the zero model with that share locked. Net2Net added explicit noise for exactly this reason. I left it out to keep growth exact, and that is my best explanation for why split trails zero. "Guided", which splits the busiest units, did worst, which fits the same story.

An LLM reading the model and writing the init did not beat leaving it empty. The fourth variant was the weight-space-programming idea in its honest form, with one difference: the MIT authors' model read raw weights as text, while mine read statistics computed from them. The LLM assistant I ran this whole project with studied the 0.5B's weight and activation statistics (which neurons fire hardest, which heads carry their group, which layers move the residual stream most, which units are near-duplicates) and wrote a plan: put the four new layers after layers 0, 12, 21 and 23 where the work is; give the busiest neurons and heads a copy that shares their reader but starts with a zero writer, so it fires from step 0 but is free to learn its own output, no twin problem; leave the rest empty. The code kept it exact; the choices were the model's. It took the early hit the model itself had predicted (the copies double the first updates on the busiest units), recovered by step 500, and then tracked zero within 0.005. Ahead of the two rules, level with leaving it empty. The plan, and the reasoning the model wrote down with it, are in the repo.

Editing weights is training, just with a different optimizer

This is the point I want to make clearly, because the weight-space programming framing hides it.

Training and weight editing are the same kind of thing: an update rule that takes the current weights plus some information and produces new weights. They differ in where the information comes from and how it is applied.

  • Gradient descent. The information comes from data, through a loss. The update is local and numeric: a batch, a gradient, a tiny step, repeated millions of times. The optimizer knows nothing about transformers. It only knows which direction lowers the loss right now.
  • An LLM editing weights. The information comes from the editor: its prior about how networks work, plus whatever it reads from the weights and activations. The edit can be structured and global. Copy this head, zero that row, rescale the norm, make this neuron fire on that feature. No data, no loss, no gradient at edit time.

So the editor is a learned optimizer. It was itself trained by gradient descent, and its edits are amortized knowledge from that training. "Without training" is only true of the moment the edit happens. The information was paid for somewhere, and there is no free lunch:

  • If the editor knows something the data would have to teach slowly, its edit is cheaper than training. That is the interesting case: known structure, known circuits, known fixes.
  • If the editor knows nothing useful, it is a worse optimizer, guessing numbers instead of measuring them.

This experiment showed both ends. The growth step is a weight edit with no training, and it is exact precisely because it adds zero information. The LLM-written plan was a weight edit informed by reading the model, and it did not beat the empty init. That is one plan, one seed and 131M tokens, from statistics rather than raw weights, so it does not show that an LLM editor cannot help. It shows that this edit gave no measurable head start over an empty init on the same tokens.

That is the core of it. Making the model bigger is free because nothing enters. Making the new part worth anything means information has to enter, from one of three places:

  1. Data, through gradient descent. Healing, and the control.
  2. Another model, through its weights or its outputs. A model writing the init, or distillation.
  3. Inference-time compute with a verifier. The model generates, something checks, only the checked part is trained on. Self-distillation.

[IMAGE 07-sources.png]

Which source is cheapest per unit of capability is the real question, and "an LLM edits the weights" is one answer to it, not a way around it.

The picture I find useful is a coach. A coach is another model: they carry a prior about how skills work, they watch the athlete (our activation statistics), and they intervene structurally. "Move your elbow", not "swing ten thousand times and keep what works". That is cheap exactly when the coach knows something the athlete would learn slowly on their own, and useless when they do not. The analogy breaks in one place, and the break is the point: a coach never touches the athlete's synapses. The instruction goes in through the senses and the athlete's own learning still has to turn it into skill. That is prompting, or curated training data. Weight editing is a surgeon who has seen many brains and rewires one directly, so the athlete wakes up with the elbow fixed and no practice happened. The growth step is that kind of surgery. So: coach for where the knowledge comes from, surgery for how it gets in.

What I take from it

  • Growth is free, exact, and empty. Anyone can double a model in a minute.
  • Healing recovers the model and then improves it, but at a billion tokens the extra capacity is worth 0.006 nats over just training the small model, and probably less than nothing per GPU hour. Practical takeaway: model growth is a bet on much larger training budgets (5B+ tokens) rather than a low-budget shortcut.
  • Filling the empty part with copies of what the small model already knows did not help, whether a rule or an LLM chose the copies, and the textbook way of doing it, exact splitting, adds no usable capacity through the copied units.
  • The experiment I would run next is the compute-matched one with a budget of 5B tokens or more, where the late gain has room to show, and where it would be worth trying noise-broken splits and the negated-gate copy (a unit that fires exactly where its donor is silent) as inits.

What went wrong along the way

For the record, because it changed the design: the first H100 host died at step 7,852 with the run's checkpoints on its local disk. I had saved only bf16 snapshots, no optimizer state, so nothing was resumable even if the disk had survived. The second attempt saved the full training state every 1,000 steps on a network volume, ran the four pilots on the full schedule so the winner could be resumed rather than restarted, and stopped its own pod when done. The resumed run reproduced the lost run's curve to the third decimal, which is the kind of boring result you want.
