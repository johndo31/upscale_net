Weight Writer

Can a small language model teach itself to write the weights of a neural network?

Status: self-chosen exploration runs in progress. Numbers as of 11 November 2026, 15:35 CET.
The short answer
  • Yes, from a checker alone, up to a point. The share of training tasks solved on the first try (the model's single most likely answer passes the check) rose from 0% to 32% in 30 rounds, and to 42% in 10 rounds with hotter sampling. Without the check, it ended at 0%.
  • Exploration decides how far the loop gets. Sampling more randomly, or simply trying more often, nearly doubled the result at round 10.
  • Blind guessing plus ordinary training does better. A model trained once on randomly generated, checked networks solves 85.4%. This task set is small enough for random search.
  • The model memorises, and this is not recursive self-improvement. Unseen tasks stay near 0%, and the gains shrink as the rounds go on.

1 · The question

Learning a skill from a checker alone

Language models normally learn from data. A recent MIT CSAIL post, Sparks of Weight-Space Programming, showed that a frontier model can write the weights of a neural network directly, as numbers, without training it.

We ask a narrower question. Can a small model, Qwen2.5-0.5B (half a billion parameters), learn that skill by itself, when the only feedback is a checker that runs what it wrote? There is no teacher model, and there are no solved examples beyond the four in the prompt.

Why it matters to us

The first experiment, On Computable Growth, ended with three places new information can come from. This experiment tests the third one, which was not run then.

2 · The task

Truth table in, weights out

The model gets a truth table and must answer with the weights of a fixed tiny network. Two-input tasks use 2 hidden units. Three-input tasks use 3.

3 · The task set

Every Boolean function of two and three inputs

The tasks are all Boolean functions of two inputs (16) and of three inputs (256). An exhaustive search confirmed that every one is solvable with small integer weights between −2 and 2.

Whole families of functions are held out together. A family is a function plus all its versions with the inputs permuted. That way the held-out tasks are really new. Parity is held out.

Two variants of the hindsight loop change only how the model explores. Hotter sampling uses temperature 1.3 instead of 0.8. Temperature controls how random the model's answers are: higher means more varied answers. More tries gives the model 64 tries per task instead of 16.

5 · Results of the loops

The loop works, and exploration decides how far it gets

With only a checker and no teacher, the model learns. The main hindsight loop went from 0% to 32.2% of training tasks solved on the first try in 30 rounds. With hotter sampling it reached 42.0% in 10 rounds, and with more tries 40.5%. After the same 10 rounds, the main loop stood at 22.9%.

Solved on the first try means that the model's single most likely answer passes the verifier. We do not sample several answers and pick the best.

The starting point was low. The base model writes a correctly shaped network 91% of the time. But it solves only 7 of 205 training tasks within 16 tries, and none of the 63 held-out tasks even with 64 tries.

The gains slowed down. The main loop sat at 31.7% from round 25 to 29 and reached 32.2% at round 30.

Pass-only never got going. It knew 7 functions in every one of its 6 rounds, because it had almost nothing to learn from. The unverified control, which keeps answers without checking them, ended at 0%.

No loop learned anything that carries over to new tasks. On the first try, every loop ended at 0% of held-out tasks. In a few single rounds one of the 63 was solved, and then lost again. Even the teacher, trained on correct solutions for every training task, solves only 7.9% of held-out tasks on the first try, and 8 of 63 (12.7%) within 16 tries.

6 · Compared with ordinary learning

Blind guessing explores better than the model does

In this setting, ordinary training on randomly generated networks, labelled by the verifier, beats every loop we ran. The model trained once on the random pool solves 85.4% of training tasks on the first try. The best loop reached 42.0%.

The reason is exploration. In a single round, random weights hit about 112 of the 205 training functions. The model's own guesses, at temperature 0.8, hit 24. After 10 rounds the count is 177 against 47.

The model also misses the harder half of the task set. 114 of the 205 training functions need two hidden units. The temperature 0.8 loop found zero of them in its first 10 rounds, and one by round 30.

The random pool we actually trained on held 176 functions. The model trained on it solves all of the functions that need one hidden unit on the first try, and 74.6% of those that need two. On held-out tasks it reaches 1.6% on the first try and 4.8% within 16 tries.

Why does blind guessing win? The task set is small. There are only 256 three-input functions, and the weights are limited to integers from −2 to 2, so random search covers most of the space quickly. A model that has just been trained mostly repeats what it already knows, so its guesses find less and less that is new.

The hoped-for advantage of the loop is that its guesses get better as the model learns. At this scale, that advantage did not show up.

7 · Is this recursive self-improvement?

No. It is self-training driven by a verifier.

Recursive self-improvement, in the strong sense, means a system that gets better at making itself better, so that each gain speeds up the next. We apply three tests. The loop fails all three.

Self-applicationDoes the system improve the process that improves it?

The loop itself (how to explore, what to keep, how to train) is fixed code that we wrote. The model only gets better at the task.

No
CompoundingDo gains feed further gains?

Gains shrink as the rounds go on and then flatten. The temperature 0.8 loop gained 11.7 points in round 1, and about 0.5 points per round at the end.

No
Open-ended measureCan the target keep growing?

The task set is fixed at 205 training functions. Once those are solved, there is nothing left to gain.

No

A real version would need the model to take over parts of its own learning process, and for that to pay off. The next section tests the smallest such step.

8 · The honest step

The model sets its own exploration

We now hand the model one part of its own learning process: how much to explore on each task. Three runs test whether its choices help. They do not: the self-chosen run ended level with random choice and with the hand-set value.

Before each answer, the model now writes one extra line, T = v. It picks a sampling temperature from a menu: 0.5, 0.8, 1.1, 1.4 or 1.7. We then sample its answer at that temperature.

One quarter of the time we replace its choice with a random menu value, so that every value keeps being tried.

The model learns its choice from what worked. We call an answer a progress event when it adds something new to the pool: a new function, or a new distinct answer for a function we already had. The temperature that produced a progress event becomes a training example for the T = line of that task. We keep at most two per task per round, from the last three rounds. So the model learns which temperature leads to progress on which task. Everything else is the hindsight loop from section 4.

Each run lasts 10 rounds. The self-chosen run succeeds if it beats random choice and matches or beats the hand-set value. We also watch whether the model picks hotter temperatures on tasks it cannot solve yet.

The extra line changes the prompt format. Compare these three runs with each other, not with the loops in section 5.

In round 2 of the self-chosen run, the model chose a mean temperature of 0.748 on tasks it had solved before and 0.802 on tasks it had never solved. The two values are almost the same, so the model learned one general setting, not a different one for each task.

Choosing did not beat chance. After 10 rounds the self-chosen run knows 89 of the 205 training functions and the random-choice run knows 92. On the first try they solve 32.2% and 38.0%. With a single run each, a gap of three functions is within what chance can produce, so we read this as a tie at best. The hand-set run knows 85 functions and solves 41.5% on the first try, the best first-try result of the three.

The model did learn something real. It started cool, with a mean choice near 0.8. From round 4 it moved to hot. By round 10 its mean choice was 1.38, and the hottest value, 1.7, was its most frequent pick. That agrees with what we found by hand in section 5: hotter sampling explores better. But the model needed four rounds to find this out, and those rounds were spent at the least productive temperatures.

The scoring rule worked against the self-chosen run early on. A progress event counts any new answer, including a new way of writing a function the model already knows, and it counts events, not events per try. Cool temperatures give more valid answers, so at the start they collected most of the credit: in round 0, 286 of 347 progress events came from 0.5 and 0.8. Measured per try in the random-choice run, 1.1 and 1.4 found the most new functions (3.8 and 3.6 per 1,000 tries) and 0.5 the fewest (1.4). A fairer rule would count only new functions, per try. We have not run that version.

9 · What it means so far

Seven findings

  1. Self-improvement from a verifier alone works. Training tasks solved on the first try went from 0% to 32% in 30 rounds, and to 42% in 10 rounds with hotter sampling. The unverified control ended at 0%, so the checking is what carries the information.
  2. Exploration is the bottleneck. Hotter sampling or more tries nearly doubled the result at round 10 (42.0% and 40.5% against 22.9%).
  3. Pass-only cannot get started. It knew 7 functions, unchanged for 6 rounds.
  4. Blind guessing plus ordinary training beats every loop here: 85.4%. The task set is small enough for random search.
  5. Nothing generalises. Held-out tasks stay near 0% in every loop, and reach only 7.9% with the teacher. The model memorises.
  6. It is not recursive self-improvement. Gains shrink as the rounds go on.
  7. Letting the model set its own exploration did not help yet. It learned by itself that hotter sampling pays, but it ended level with picking temperatures at random and with a hand-set value (89 functions against 92 and 85), and it chose the same temperature for solved and unsolved tasks.

Each result comes from a single run with one random seed.
